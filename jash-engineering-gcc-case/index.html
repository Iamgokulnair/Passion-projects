Situation
Jash Engineering is a ₹736 Cr Indian water-infrastructure group that just doubled in size — and is now finding out what that costs
Jash is a promoter-led manufacturer out of Indore that's become India's market leader in water-control gates and one of the top five globally, selling into 45+ countries. It got there the way most Indian engineering companies do: by winning the next order and standing up whatever entity was needed to deliver it, not by sitting down first to design an operating model. That's a completely normal way to grow. It's also why, ten legal entities later, the back office hasn't caught up to the front book.

The Options
Three routes cover the sourcing decision completely — and none of the three overlap
MECE
Mutually Exclusive,
Collectively Exhaustive

Every way of sourcing support work is either done by a vendor you don't own (outsourcing), a vendor-built asset you take over later (BOT), or an entity you own from day one (captive). There's no fourth meaningfully different route, and no two of these three overlap — each answers "who owns the entity and who carries the risk" differently, and every option answers it exactly once.

8
Why — Part 2
BOT's entire value is buying local build capability Jash already owns — and pure outsourcing puts IP-touching work at arm's length
Markup exposure over time, by route (illustrative)
Rejected — both of them
Named and defended, not waved away

BOT is the right answer for a company that doesn't already have India — it converts a partner's local build capability into your own asset, on a schedule, without you having to hire the first hundred people yourself. Against BOT for Jash — that value is buying capability Jash already has. Handed over later at a markup, it earns its keep only when you're starting from zero — Jash isn't.

Against pure outsourcing — it puts IP- and customer-touching work at arm's length without a real unit-cost edge once you already own the entity that could do it captively — and permanently pays the 25–45% markup.

Where outsourcing IS kept: transactional, non-IP-touching finance work — AP processing, payroll, expense audit. A deliberate carve-out, not an oversight.
Naming the rejected alternatives, not just the chosen path, is what separates a recommendation from a foregone conclusion.

Cash-Flow & Tax Mechanics
The 25–45% markup that hurts on Slide 5 — disappears entirely once the work is intercompany
If this were a vendor

An unrelated outsourcing vendor prices at cost plus a 25–45% margin (Slide 5) — and the group pays that margin, in cash, forever. That markup is real money leaving the group every year.

Because it's Jash serving Jash

The India centre still has to charge its own overseas entities a service fee — India's transfer-pricing rules require an arm's-length cost-plus markup, illustratively ~15% (in line with the market range for captive service centres) — but that fee is intercompany.

The consolidation insight — the mechanic a client team actually needs to see: in Jash's consolidated group accounts, the India centre's fee income and the overseas entity's fee expense are related-party balances that eliminate on consolidation. The illustrative 15% cost-plus that satisfies the tax authority never shows up as a real group cost — only the underlying spend does. Run through outsourcing's markup, that same money is gone for good. That gap — not just "who owns the entity" — is what the ₹11 Cr saving is actually made of.
₹9 Cr investment split ~₹6 Cr capex · ~₹3 Cr opex Systems/fit-out vs dual-running — illustrative
Intercompany markup ~15% cost-plus, eliminates on consolidation
Vendor markup (for contrast) 25–45%, real cash cost, forever
Basis: transfer-pricing mechanic is a general intercompany-accounting principle, illustrated with Jash's figures. The 15% cost-plus rate is a stated illustrative assumption, not a sourced Jash rate — no company-specific transfer-pricing policy is public.
