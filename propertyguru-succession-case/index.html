Today — how stale the answer gets

Known once a year. A successor rated "ready" can go up to twelve months without the rating being touched.

Redesign — how stale the answer gets

Known continuously. Never more than one calibration cycle out of date, because the trigger is a real change, not a date on the calendar.

Embedded, not addedThe product wraps the calibration conversation managers already run — it is not a new destination. Success looks like a manager spending less time preparing than before, and a successor who can state their own tier and next action without being asked.
Each stage of today's cycle fails a different group — which is why fixing any one stage in isolation has never worked. The friction removed on the future side is a direct answer to the failure named above it.

04 · AI-Enabled SolutionSlide 6 of 9

AI enters at four points in the process — and the greatest value is not the obvious one

Then — what AI actually does, and what each use case prevents
Drafts the brief

Before every calibration.
Prevents walking in cold, reconstructing from memory.

Monthly touchpoint

Surfaces readiness between cycles.
Prevents candidates falling through the cracks.

Flags a competitor move

Alerts on newly-credible candidates.
Prevents missing an unnominated successor.

Debriefs bench coverage

Maps eligible share by market.
Prevents thin bench found only when a role empties.

Where the greatest value sits

Identification and monitoring — not preparation. Drafting saves time; scanning and monitoring hold the whole population in view continuously, which no manager can do alone. Preparation ships first because it's cheapest to prove, not because it's worth most.

Where human judgement always stays

Who is ready · who needs another year · who gets the next stretch assignment. Every drafted tier shows its evidence, and the manager edits the reasoning before it's used.

Governance, privacy & bias — tied to the step

2–3: only what a manager already entered. 4: visible to that manager, that group, that successor only. 5: every override logged and reviewed at the gate — never fed to a self-modifying model.

And a wider point: succession is not only about promotion. It is equally about making sure one person's absence does not damage a team — continuity cover — and supporting the person moving into the new role.
Because the draft works from what one manager enters rather than a model trained on company-wide history, the sharper bias risk is a manager's own framing carrying forward unexamined — the calibration group is the real check.

05 · Delivery & ImplementationSlide 7 of 9

Ninety days, two decision gates, and a pilot cheap enough to stop

How we prioritise which function goes first
Pilot · testing · iterate before scaling
Pilot approach

One function, entirely by hand — no new software. First ninety days on Talent Development's own time: a single lead, part-time, no vendor spend.

Testing & feedback loop

A debrief with the pilot manager immediately after — not a survey sent later. Three questions: what took less time, what still felt manual, what they'd keep.

The bar for Gate 2 — expand only if two of three hold

Prep took less time · a successor got a real, dated action · the manager would recommend it unasked. If none hold: adjust and run one more cycle, not add software. Two cycles without signs is the point to stop.

Each gate is a real decision, not a formality. If it does not work, nothing about the existing annual process has been touched — a stopped pilot costs a few weeks of one team's attention and leaves nothing to unwind.

Why the split mattersLeading indicators tell us whether the mechanics are catching on; lagging indicators tell us whether it was worth catching on to. Reading only the first is premature; waiting only for the second means flying blind for months.
Together these guard against the two common failure modes: a process that looks busy but changes nothing underneath it, and one that looks efficient but produces worse decisions.

Change management & adoption strategy
1 · Prove before promoting

Communication is quiet early. One team uses it for real first, so everything said afterwards is grounded in something that happened.

2 · Sequence the audiences

Sponsors before discovery · pilot team at start · calibration group at pilot end · everyone else only once the model is stable.

3 · Make the manager the beneficiary

Adoption is driven by the first calibration taking less time than the last one — not by mandate.

4 · Hold sponsors to three fixed points

Discovery complete · first calibration · decision gate. A manager who won't participate is a conversation with their leader — framed as business risk, never compliance.

Not incidental — the design. Every fix above costs nothing to try and nothing to reverse. None of the six risks are answered by buying something or asking for headcount; each is answered by how the pilot is framed, sequenced, or reviewed.
The end stateSuccession planning stops being something that happens to a manager once a year, and becomes how we know, at any point, who is ready — until nobody thinks of it as a separate initiative at all.
A process that depends on goodwill from stretched managers or sceptical leaders will not survive its first difficult quarter. Nothing existing is switched off until the new model has completed two consecutive successful cycles.
